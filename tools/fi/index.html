🏁 When Could I Stop?

Multiply a year of spending by twenty-five and you get the pile that, by the historical record, would have funded it indefinitely. That multiplier is more contested than it sounds — so this shows the target as a range, works out when you would reach it, and lets you see what one permanent change in spending does to the date. It moves it further than almost anyone expects.

The assumptions

i

4% comes from the Trinity Study (1998). It tested historical 30-year US portfolios and found a 4% initial withdrawal, raised with inflation, survived nearly all of them. It is a historical success rate, not a promise — and later work, notably Wade Pfau's, argues for 3–3.5% when yields are low or the retirement is longer than the thirty years the study actually measured.

Every figure here is in today's money, so the return is a real one. Around 7% is the long-run historical average for a broad stock index.

    What this means

      The 4% rule is a historical success frequency drawn from 30-year US market history, not a guarantee about the next thirty years — sequence-of-returns risk in the first decade matters more to the outcome than the average return does, and a retirement longer than thirty years is asking the study a question it never answered. Every figure is in today's money, with no tax on withdrawals and no allowance for a market that misbehaves at the wrong moment. This is not financial advice, and nothing you type leaves your device.
